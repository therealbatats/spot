Fields Viewer How it works

Every image here is real data from NASA's SPHEREx space telescope, which maps the whole sky in infrared light every six months. Pick a patch of sky, then flip between dates. Stars far away stay put. Nearby stars and brown dwarfs drift. Some stars change brightness. Anything drawn by us is labelled SIMULATED.

What am I looking at?
Brightness over time (each detector band)

Brightness of the target in each visit's added image, shown as a change relative to that band's own average, so all six bands fit on one chart. A flat line means no change. Hollow symbols: the star is so bright that its centre is saturated, so we measure its unsaturated outer glow ("wings") instead; this is less precise (tested on Barnard's Star, it scatters by about 2–37% between visits).
How big must a change be to count? In our control galaxy (M101), where nothing should change, this method still wobbles by 2–6% between visits. So treat changes below about 10% as noise, and be suspicious of a jump that shows up in only one band.

Spectrum of the target, by date (made on the fly from the frames)

Each dot is one SPHEREx frame: brightness of the target measured in a 12-arcsecond circle, plotted against that frame's wavelength. Colours = dates. If the colours separate, the object changed between visits. Fluxes are not aperture-corrected and are for comparison between dates only.

Provenance: every frame used

All frames are SPHEREx Level-2 spectral images. "Source" opens the original file at NASA/IPAC's archive (large file, ~65 MB). Flags show how many pixels SPHEREx's own pipeline marked as unreliable.

Planet X simulator SIMULATED

A far-away planet does not sit still. As Earth (and SPHEREx with it) circles the Sun, the planet seems to shift back and forth (parallax), and it creeps slowly along its own orbit. Turn this on to draw a pretend planet into these real images, at the distance you choose.

Could SPHEREx really see Planet X?

Probably not. A hypothetical "Planet Nine" a few hundred times farther from the Sun than Earth would shine only by faint reflected sunlight: published estimates put it around visual magnitude 22–25 near the far point of its orbit. SPHEREx's sensitivity per wavelength channel is about magnitude 18.4–19.5 (AB), so a planet like that would be below the noise in a single SPHEREx image. Our simulated dot is drawn much brighter than a real one would be, so you can learn what its motion looks like. Sources: see How it works.
